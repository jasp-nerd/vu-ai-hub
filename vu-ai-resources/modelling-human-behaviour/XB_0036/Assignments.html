    3. Formulating hypotheses

    A model can be used for different purposes. One of these purposes is analysis of the phenomenon. A common way to perform such an analysis is to formulate hypotheses or “properties” that should be validated against a model simulation trace.

    Formulate three hypotheses or research questions about the effect of some medication regime (time points, size of pill, max/min value) on a specific person (blood volume, half-life time). Make sure that you formulate the hypotheses or research questions in a scientific and precise manner: your intentions should be so clear, that someone else could set up the simulation experiment and answer them for you. (For each hypothesis or research question, also provide a motivation.)

    Advices:

    • Formulate as precise as possible (i.e., make all parameters explicit).
    • The research questions should be as diverse as possible (e.g., not “how often will the medication level for a specific person be below 40” and “how often will the medication level for another person be below 50”).
    • Think about different combinations of parameters, but also different types of checks (e.g., % of time above max, difference between frequent small doses or less frequent large doses, effect of missing a pill, etc).

    4. Validating hypotheses

    What is stress?

    Stress or burn-out is common problem in modern society. Although in the basis stress is a natural reaction, prolonged periods of stress can cause serious (mental) health problems. According to recent research, up to 1 out of the 7 employees in the Netherlands has symptoms of a burn-out.

    The mechanism behind stress is described in an online article[1] and is summarized here. Stress is your body’s way of responding to any kind of demand or threat. When you sense danger—whether it’s real or imagined—the body’s defenses kick into high gear in a rapid, automatic process known as the “fight-or-flight” reaction or the “stress response”. The stress response is the body’s way of protecting you. When working properly, it helps you stay focused, energetic, and alert. But beyond a certain point, stress stops being helpful and starts causing major damage to your health, mood, productivity, relationships, and your quality of life.

    If you tend to get stressed out frequently, like many of us in today’s demanding world, your body may exist in a heightened state of stress most of the time, in other words, your chronic stress level becomes high. And that can lead to serious health problems. A high chronic stress level disrupts nearly every system in your body, i.e. it leads to a disrupted cognitive system, a disrupted emotional system, and disrupted physical systems such as your immune, digestive and reproductive systems. It can even rewire the brain, increasing your vulnerability to anxiety, depression, and other mental health problems. The disruption of each of these systems leads to specific cognitive symptoms, emotional symptoms and physical symptoms, and together the malfunctioning of these systems is indicated via behavioral symptoms.

    Not all people are as vulnerable for developing chronic stress as others. People with a high vulnerability, e.g. people that worry excessively about something that may or may not happen, or have irrational, pessimistic thoughts about life, will more quickly develop a high chronic stress level.

    Finally, whether a threat leads to a stress response depends, at least in part, on your perception of it. Something that’s stressful to you may not faze someone else; they may even enjoy it. While some of us are terrified of getting up in front of people to perform or speak, for example, others live for the spotlight.
