2. Performing simulations with an Excel model

A model of the level of medication in the blood of a patient given in: Assignment1-medicine.xlsx (use the "Random factor" sheet). The model’s output is the amount of the relevant drug in the blood of a patient (mg/l).

You can change the output of the model by changing one of the parameters in the yellow cells of the Excel-file:

  • time points when pill is taken
  • size of the pill (mg)
  • half-life time of a pill in a person (basic decay factor), influenced by a (random) activity level
  • blood volume of a person (liter)
  • minimum value for being effective / maximum safe value

Because there is some randomness involved (i.e. the basic decay factor is slightly adapted every time), you will get different outputs when you re-run the model. You can force this by changing something in the Excel-file, e.g. delete the empty A1-cell (select the cell and press "del").

  1. Adapt the blood volume to the volume of a young child, i.e. 3 liters. Describe what you observe with respect to the medication level in the blood compared with that of the value of an adult.
  2. Keep blood volume at 3 liters. Change the maximum value to 210 mg/l. Rerun the model 10 times. What is the average of the number of times that the level is above the maximum during the simulation period of 110 hours?
  3. Change the maximum value back to 120 mg/l.
    • Describe three ways in which you can reduce the number of times that the blood level is above the maximum for this child.
    • Implement one of these ways and describe which values you have chosen so that the is almost never above the maximum.

3. Formulating hypotheses

A model can be used for different purposes. One of these purposes is analysis of the phenomenon. A common way to perform such an analysis is to formulate hypotheses or “properties” that should be validated against a model simulation trace.

Formulate three hypotheses or research questions about the effect of some medication regime (time points, size of pill, max/min value) on a specific person (blood volume, half-life time). Make sure that you formulate the hypotheses or research questions in a scientific and precise manner: your intentions should be so clear, that someone else could set up the simulation experiment and answer them for you. (For each hypothesis or research question, also provide a motivation.)

Advices:

  • Formulate as precise as possible (i.e., make all parameters explicit).
  • The research questions should be as diverse as possible (e.g., not “how often will the medication level for a specific person be below 40” and “how often will the medication level for another person be below 50”).
  • Think about different combinations of parameters, but also different types of checks (e.g., % of time above max, difference between frequent small doses or less frequent large doses, effect of missing a pill, etc).

4. Validating hypotheses

Now, use the model to validate two of your hypotheses or research questions. You may choose them yourself. Change the parameters in the yellow cells (there is probably no need to change other cells) and look at the effects. For each research question, you have to include a picture of the simulation in your document and write a paragraph about what you can conclude, whether that is in line with your expectations, and what might be an explanation for an outcome that is different from what you expected.
